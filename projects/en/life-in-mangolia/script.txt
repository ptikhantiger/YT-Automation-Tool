LIFE IN MONGOLIA
How People Live in the World's Most Extreme Land of Nomads
Imagine living in a country where there are more horses than people.
Where winter temperatures can fall far below freezing.
Where a family can live inside a round felt tent called a ger, surrounded by nothing but grassland stretching toward the horizon.
Where the nearest neighbor might be many kilometers away.
And where surviving the winter is not just difficult.
It can determine whether an entire family has a future.
This is Mongolia.
A vast land between Russia and China, famous for endless grasslands, powerful horses, the Gobi Desert, and one of the last great nomadic cultures on Earth.
But Mongolia is changing.
Today, modern apartment towers rise above Ulaanbaatar while thousands of families still live with animals on open pasture.
Young people use smartphones and social media while their parents continue traditions that have survived for generations.
And every year, Mongolian families face one of the harshest climates on Earth.
So what is everyday life really like in Mongolia?
How do people survive the freezing winters?
What do they eat?
How do children grow up?
Why are horses so important?
And why do some Mongolians still choose a nomadic life when modern cities are only a few hours away?
This is the story of life in Mongolia.
THE LAND OF THE BLUE SKY
Mongolia is enormous.
It is one of the world's largest landlocked countries, yet only a few million people live across this huge territory.
Much of the country is covered by open steppe, mountains, dry valleys and desert.
There are no large oceans here.
No tropical forests.
No dense network of cities.
Instead, the landscape seems to continue forever.
You can drive for hours and see almost nothing except grass, rocks, mountains and the occasional herd of animals.
This enormous space has shaped Mongolian culture.
For centuries, people learned how to move with the seasons instead of trying to completely control the land.
The result was one of the world's great pastoral cultures.
THE NOMADIC LIFE
For a Mongolian herder, home does not always mean one permanent address.
A family may move between seasonal camps depending on weather, pasture and water.
Their home is often a ger.
From the outside, a ger looks simple.
It is circular, portable and covered with layers of felt.
But inside, it can be surprisingly comfortable.
There may be beds around the walls, a small stove in the center, storage cabinets, rugs and family belongings.
The circular design is practical.
The ger can be assembled and taken apart relatively quickly, allowing families to move with their animals.
For a nomadic family, the ger is more than a house.
It is the center of family life.
It is where children sleep.
Where food is prepared.
Where guests are welcomed.
Where stories are told.
And where families try to stay warm when temperatures outside become dangerously low.
THE ANIMALS ARE EVERYTHING
Mongolian herders depend heavily on their animals.
Sheep.
Goats.
Cattle.
Horses.
And camels.
These animals provide food, transportation, materials and income.
A herd is not simply property.
It can represent a family's entire livelihood.
Sheep provide meat and wool.
Goats provide cashmere.
Cattle provide milk and meat.
Horses provide transportation and milk.
Camels are especially valuable in the Gobi region.
This is why a Mongolian herder watches the weather so carefully.
One bad winter can destroy years of work.
And this brings us to one of Mongolia's greatest challenges.
THE MONGOLIAN WINTER
Mongolian winters can be brutal.
Temperatures can fall dramatically below freezing.
Snow and ice can cover the grass.
Water can freeze.
Animals can struggle to find food.
And strong winds can make an already dangerous environment even harder.
But the most feared winter phenomenon has a special name.
Dzud.
A dzud occurs when extreme winter conditions make it difficult or impossible for livestock to reach enough food.
Sometimes there is deep snow.
Sometimes ice covers the grass.
Sometimes an extremely dry summer leaves animals with insufficient reserves before winter arrives.
And sometimes several bad conditions happen together.
For herders, this can become a disaster.
An animal that cannot reach grass becomes weak.
A weak animal is more vulnerable to cold.
And when thousands of animals die, the family's livelihood can disappear with them.
This is why Mongolian life is closely connected to nature.
The weather is not simply something people check on their phones.
It can decide everything.
CHILDREN OF THE STEPPE
For children growing up in rural Mongolia, childhood can look very different from childhood in a large Western city.
There may be no crowded neighborhood.
No shopping mall nearby.
No traffic outside the house.
Instead, children grow up surrounded by animals and open land.
They learn practical skills early.
How to ride a horse.
How to care for animals.
How to help around the ger.
How to understand weather.
And how to move safely across the landscape.
A young Mongolian child can become comfortable on horseback at an age when children elsewhere are still learning to ride a bicycle.
But education is also important.
Many rural children attend schools in district centers or towns.
Some children from remote herding families live in school dormitories during the academic year and return home during holidays.
This creates a unique childhood.
Part of the year may be spent in a classroom.
Another part may be spent helping the family on the steppe.
THE HORSE
You cannot understand Mongolia without understanding the horse.
Horses are deeply connected to Mongolian history and identity.
The horse was essential to the Mongol Empire and to life across the steppe.
Even today, horses remain important in rural communities.
They are transportation.
They are part of celebrations.
They are used in traditional competitions.
And they remain a powerful symbol of Mongolian culture.
Mongolian horses are generally smaller and stockier than many modern racing breeds, but they are extremely adapted to the harsh environment.
They can survive in conditions where other animals might struggle.
They can find food beneath snow.
And they can travel long distances across the steppe.
For Mongolian children, learning to ride can be part of growing up.
And during the famous Naadam festival, horse racing becomes one of the country's most spectacular traditions.
NAADAM
Every summer, Mongolia celebrates Naadam.
It is one of the country's most important national festivals.
The traditional competitions center around three major sports.
Horse racing.
Wrestling.
And archery.
But Naadam is more than sport.
It is a celebration of Mongolian identity.
Families gather.
People wear traditional clothing.
Music fills the air.
Horses race across enormous open spaces.
Wrestlers compete in colorful traditional costumes.
And archers demonstrate extraordinary skill.
For visitors, Naadam can look like a spectacular festival.
For Mongolians, it is connected to history, culture and national identity.
FOOD IN MONGOLIA
Mongolian food has been shaped by the environment.
When you live in a cold climate and depend heavily on livestock, your traditional diet naturally becomes rich in meat and dairy.
Mutton is extremely important.
Beef and goat meat are also common.
In some regions, horse meat is eaten as well.
Milk is transformed into many different products.
There is yogurt.
Cheese.
Cream.
Butter.
And fermented milk drinks.
One famous traditional drink is airag, made from fermented mare's milk.
For someone unfamiliar with it, the taste can be surprising.
But for Mongolians, it is an important part of traditional culture.
Another popular food is buuz.
These are steamed dumplings filled with meat.
Families often prepare large quantities of them, especially during celebrations and holidays.
There is also khuushuur, a fried meat pastry that is particularly popular during Naadam.
Mongolian food may not always look complicated.
But it is designed for a harsh environment.
It provides energy.
It is filling.
And it uses ingredients that are available in the local landscape.
THE GOBI DESERT
Then there is the Gobi.
When people hear the word Mongolia, they often imagine endless grassland.
But Mongolia is also home to one of the world's great deserts.
The Gobi stretches across southern Mongolia and northern China.
It is not simply a sea of sand.
Large parts of the Gobi are covered by rock, gravel, dry plains and mountains.
The environment is extremely harsh.
Summers can be very hot.
Winters can be bitterly cold.
Water can be scarce.
Yet people have lived here for generations.
The Gobi is also home to remarkable wildlife, including wild camels and other animals adapted to extreme conditions.
For the people living here, the desert is not an empty place.
It is home.
LIFE IN ULAANBAATAR
But Mongolia is not only nomadic camps and deserts.
Almost half of the country's population lives in and around Ulaanbaatar, the capital.
The city is completely different from the open steppe.
There are apartment towers.
Universities.
Shopping centers.
Restaurants.
Traffic.
Schools.
Businesses.
Technology companies.
And young people dressed in modern fashion.
Walk through central Ulaanbaatar and you might see smartphones, coffee shops and modern buildings.
But just outside the center, another Mongolia appears.
Huge ger districts spread across the hills surrounding the city.
Many families live in gers because of housing costs and migration from rural areas.
This creates an unusual contrast.
Modern apartment buildings in the center.
Traditional gers on the outskirts.
The old Mongolia and the new Mongolia existing almost side by side.
THE AIR PROBLEM
Ulaanbaatar also faces one of its most serious problems during winter.
Air pollution.
The city is located in a valley, and during the coldest months, smoke can become trapped over the city.
Many households in ger districts rely on heating systems that can produce significant smoke.
Combined with traffic and other sources, winter air pollution can become severe.
For families with young children, this is a major concern.
So while Mongolia has some of the cleanest and emptiest landscapes on Earth outside its cities, its capital faces the opposite problem.
Too many people.
Too much smoke.
And not enough clean winter air.
THE MODERN YOUNG GENERATION
Mongolia's young generation lives between two worlds.
A teenager may spend part of the summer helping with animals on the family pasture.
Then return to Ulaanbaatar and attend university.
They may know how to ride a horse and also use advanced technology.
They might listen to traditional music one day and international pop music the next.
Social media has changed how young Mongolians communicate.
The internet connects remote communities to the rest of the world.
And many young people dream of careers outside traditional herding.
Technology, education and urbanization are changing the country.
But this does not necessarily mean that traditional culture will disappear.
Instead, many young Mongolians are trying to combine both worlds.
THE MONGOLIAN FAMILY
Family remains extremely important in Mongolian society.
In rural areas, family members often work together.
Herding animals is not a nine-to-five job.
Someone must check the animals.
Someone must prepare food.
Someone must collect fuel.
Someone must care for children.
Someone must watch the weather.
And during difficult seasons, everyone has to help.
Life can be physically demanding.
But it can also create a powerful sense of cooperation.
When guests arrive at a ger, hospitality is important.
A visitor may be offered tea or food even when the family lives far from the nearest town.
This tradition comes from a world where travelers depended on the kindness of strangers.
THE WOMEN OF MONGOLIA
Mongolian women have played important roles in both traditional and modern society.
In rural communities, women often contribute heavily to animal care, food production, household responsibilities and family income.
In cities, women are highly visible in education, business, healthcare, government and professional careers.
Mongolian women also face the challenges that come with a rapidly changing society.
Urbanization, economic inequality and changing family expectations have created new pressures.
But the image of Mongolian women is not limited to tradition.
Many are highly educated, ambitious and active in modern professional life.
THE LEGACY OF THE MONGOL EMPIRE
More than eight centuries ago, Mongolian armies created one of the largest contiguous empires in history.
The name most closely associated with that empire is Genghis Khan.
Today, his legacy remains visible throughout Mongolia.
His name appears on buildings, businesses, products and monuments.
A huge statue complex outside Ulaanbaatar shows Genghis Khan on horseback.
For Mongolians, the historical legacy is complicated but powerful.
The empire is part of the country's national story.
It represents military power, mobility and the ability of people from the steppe to influence a huge part of the world.
But modern Mongolia is not the Mongol Empire.
It is a democratic country trying to build a modern economy while preserving its cultural identity.
FROM SOCIALISM TO MODERN MONGOLIA
For most of the twentieth century, Mongolia was closely aligned with the Soviet Union.
The country followed a socialist political and economic system.
That period deeply influenced architecture, education, politics and urban life.
Then, in the early 1990s, Mongolia underwent a peaceful democratic transition.
The country moved toward a market economy and democratic political institutions.
The transformation was difficult.
But it opened Mongolia to the wider world.
Today, Mongolia balances relationships with its two enormous neighbors, Russia and China, while also maintaining strong connections with countries around the world.
MINING AND THE NEW ECONOMY
Modern Mongolia's economy depends heavily on natural resources.
The country has major deposits of coal, copper and other minerals.
Mining has brought enormous economic opportunities.
But it has also created difficult questions.
How much should Mongolia depend on mining?
How can the country protect its environment?
How should mining wealth be distributed?
And what happens to traditional herding communities when landscapes change?
These questions are becoming increasingly important as Mongolia tries to develop without losing the environment that has supported nomadic life for centuries.
A LIFE BETWEEN TWO WORLDS
Perhaps the most fascinating thing about Mongolia is that two very different ways of life can exist at the same time.
In one part of the country, a herder can wake before sunrise, open the door of a ger and look across thousands of kilometers of empty landscape.
A few hours away, a university student can sit inside a modern cafe in Ulaanbaatar using a laptop and smartphone.
One family may depend on horses and sheep.
Another may work in finance or technology.
One child may spend the summer riding across the steppe.
Another may grow up surrounded by apartment buildings.
Yet both are part of the same Mongolia.
THE PRICE OF LIVING IN THE EXTREME
Mongolia's beauty comes with a price.
The country is far from major global markets.
Its population is small and widely spread.
Infrastructure is difficult to build across enormous distances.
Winter can be dangerous.
Water can be scarce.
Pastures can deteriorate.
Extreme weather can destroy livestock.
And the economy can be vulnerable to changes in commodity prices.
For rural families, climate change adds another layer of uncertainty.
Warmer temperatures, changing rainfall and increasingly difficult weather patterns can affect pasture and livestock.
For a culture built around animals and seasonal movement, environmental change is not an abstract idea.
It can directly affect whether a family can remain on the land.
WHY PEOPLE STAY
So why stay?
Why would anyone choose to live in a place where winters are so cold, distances are so great and life can be so physically demanding?
For many Mongolians, the answer is simple.
This is home.
The grassland is home.
The horses are home.
The mountains are home.
The ger is home.
The family is home.
There is a freedom in being able to look across the horizon and see almost nothing but nature.
There is a sense of identity connected to the land.
And there is pride in a culture that survived centuries of change.
THE FUTURE OF MONGOLIA
Mongolia is changing faster than ever.
Cities are expanding.
Young people are moving toward urban areas.
Technology is spreading.
Mining is transforming the economy.
Tourism is growing.
And traditional herding is becoming harder in some regions.
But the country's identity has not disappeared.
A horse still means something.
A ger still means something.
Naadam still matters.
Family still matters.
The steppe still matters.
And the knowledge passed from one generation to the next remains valuable.
Maybe the future of Mongolia will not be about choosing between tradition and modernity.
Maybe it will be about finding a way to keep both.
THE REAL MONGOLIA
Mongolia is often shown as an empty country.
A place of deserts, horses and endless grasslands.
But Mongolia is not empty.
It is full of stories.
Inside every ger is a family.
Behind every herd is generations of knowledge.
Behind every horse is a culture that has depended on mobility for centuries.
Behind every city street is a young generation deciding what Mongolia should become.
This is a country where children can learn to ride before they fully understand how special that skill is.
Where a family can live thousands of kilometers from a major city and still connect to the world through a smartphone.
Where traditional food is still prepared in modern kitchens.
Where ancient history is celebrated alongside modern technology.
And where survival is not about conquering nature.
It is about learning how to live with it.
When the winter arrives, Mongolians prepare.
When the grass grows, animals move.
When the summer festival comes, horses race.
When guests arrive, tea is served.
And when the horizon stretches endlessly in every direction, life continues.
This is Mongolia.
A country of extreme weather.
Endless landscapes.
Powerful traditions.
Modern cities.
Hard winters.
Fast change.
And people who have learned one of the most important lessons on Earth.
You do not always need to control nature.
Sometimes you simply need to understand it.
This is Extreme Earth Life.

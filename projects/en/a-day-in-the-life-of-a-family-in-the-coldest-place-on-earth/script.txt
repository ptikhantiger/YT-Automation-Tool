A DAY IN THE LIFE OF A FAMILY IN THE COLDEST PLACE ON EARTH
−71°C in Yakutia
Imagine waking up in a place where the temperature outside can fall to −71 degrees Celsius.
At these temperatures, going outside is no longer a normal part of life.
It becomes a carefully planned task.
A simple walk to school can become dangerous.
Water cannot simply come from a tap.
And the warmth inside your home depends on a fire that must never be allowed to go out.
This is Yakutia, one of the coldest inhabited regions on Earth.
Here, winter can dominate the landscape for months.
The rivers freeze.
The ground becomes extremely hard.
The air hurts your face within seconds.
And families have to prepare for the cold long before it arrives.
But this is not simply a story about surviving freezing temperatures.
It is a story about family.
About children going to school.
About parents preparing food.
About fathers teaching traditional skills to their sons.
About fishing through thick ice.
And about finding warmth in the simplest things.
Today, we are following one family through an ordinary winter day in Yakutia.
THE FIRE THAT KEEPS THE FAMILY ALIVE
It is early morning.
While most of the world is still asleep, the first job inside this home has already begun.
The wood-burning stove needs attention.
For almost nine months of the year, the fire can burn day and night.
There is no room for carelessness.
If the fire goes out during an extreme winter night, the house can lose its warmth surprisingly quickly.
Wood has to be prepared.
Logs have to be brought inside.
And someone must always be ready to add more fuel.
For this family, the stove is more than a way to heat the house.
It is the center of winter life.
It keeps the rooms warm.
It makes cooking possible.
It protects the family from the cold outside.
And every morning, the same routine begins again.
FIRE IS NOT THE ONLY ESSENTIAL
There is another problem that most people never think about.
Water.
In a normal city, you turn on a tap and water appears.
Here, winter changes everything.
Underground pipes can freeze solid.
So families in remote communities have traditionally depended on frozen rivers and stored ice.
Before winter becomes too severe, large blocks of ice can be cut from the river and brought home.
The frozen blocks are then used as a source of water throughout the winter.
Imagine needing to collect your drinking water before the winter becomes too cold.
For people living here, this is not an adventure.
It is simply preparation.
BREAKFAST IN THE EXTREME COLD
While the fire keeps burning, breakfast is being prepared.
The food is simple, but it is designed for a cold climate.
Dairy products have traditionally played an important role in the local diet.
A sweet breakfast made with cream and homemade jam gives the family energy before another freezing day begins.
Outside, the temperature may be far below anything most people have ever experienced.
Inside, the family eats together.
For a few minutes, the cold outside does not matter.
But one member of the family still has somewhere to go.
School.
MEETING THE YOUNGEST SON
The youngest son is nine years old.
Like children everywhere, he would probably prefer to stay in bed a little longer.
But this is a school morning.
Winter does not automatically cancel education here.
Schools can continue operating even when temperatures reach around −40 degrees Celsius.
Only when conditions become dangerously extreme can schools close.
That means the morning routine begins early.
Wash.
Eat.
Get dressed.
And prepare for the journey outside.
But in Yakutia, getting dressed is not about looking good.
IT IS ABOUT SURVIVAL.
DRESSING FOR −40°C
The boy begins putting on his winter clothing.
Thermal layers first.
Then insulated trousers.
Heavy boots.
Thick outer clothing.
Gloves.
And finally, enough protection to cover as much exposed skin as possible.
His mother carefully checks everything.
There cannot be a mistake.
At temperatures this low, exposed skin can become vulnerable to frostbite very quickly.
The face needs particular protection.
Every gap matters.
Every layer has a purpose.
This is something children here learn from an early age.
When the weather becomes this extreme, clothing is not fashion.
It is protection.
THE JOURNEY TO SCHOOL
The door opens.
And the difference is immediate.
The warmth of the house disappears behind him.
The cold air hits his face.
His breath becomes visible.
Tiny crystals can begin forming around eyelashes and clothing.
At around −40 degrees, even a short walk can feel incredibly long.
The school is only about ten minutes away.
But ten minutes in this environment is not the same as ten minutes somewhere else.
The boy keeps moving.
His face is covered.
His hands are protected.
His footsteps continue through the frozen landscape.
And finally, the school appears.
For his family, this short journey is something they have learned to respect.
THE SCHOOL IN YAKUTIA
Inside the school, everything feels different.
Warmth.
Light.
Classrooms.
Teachers.
Other children.
For a moment, the brutal winter outside feels distant.
The class is relatively small.
Students spend hours studying subjects such as mathematics, literature, art, geography, history and languages.
Children in Yakutia can learn Russian, English and Sakha, the local language associated with the region's indigenous culture.
This is another side of life here.
Yakutia may look incredibly remote, but education connects children to a much larger world.
Some children from families in remote areas may eventually leave their villages to study in larger towns or cities.
Education can give them choices that previous generations did not have.
A MORNING BREAK
After the first lessons, it is time to eat again.
In extreme cold, food provides more than comfort.
The body needs energy to maintain its temperature.
A simple meal can help children get through the long winter day.
But outside, playing is difficult.
Going outdoors means putting on all those layers again.
So during the coldest periods, children spend much of their free time indoors.
Eventually, the school day ends.
And it is time to go home.
THE WALK HOME
On the way back, the boy meets a friend.
And suddenly, the freezing landscape becomes a little less serious.
They stop at a local shop.
And despite the temperature outside, there is one thing children still love.
Ice cream.
It may seem strange to eat something frozen when the air itself is colder than most household freezers.
But children are still children.
They laugh.
They talk.
They play.
And then they continue toward home.
In a small community, people know one another.
The village is home to only a small population, creating a close-knit environment where families recognize each other and children can move around with a sense of familiarity.
HOME IN THE FROZEN LAND
For many families here, home is a wooden house built to withstand the winter.
There is no need for a complicated luxury.
What matters is insulation.
Strength.
And warmth.
Firewood becomes one of the most important resources in the household.
Families prepare it long before the coldest months arrive.
Every gap in the walls matters.
Every piece of wood has value.
And every evening, someone must make sure the stove continues burning.
Outside, another remarkable resident of this frozen land is waiting.
THE HORSES THAT CAN SURVIVE THE COLD
Yakutian horses are famous for their ability to survive in extreme conditions.
They have thick winter coats and are adapted to the harsh environment.
During winter, they can search beneath snow for vegetation.
For people who depend on animals, this ability is incredibly important.
Animals are not simply part of the landscape.
They are connected to food, work and survival.
THE OUTDOOR TOILET
Then comes one of the most unusual parts of everyday life for someone from a warm climate.
The bathroom.
In many remote villages, there is no central sewage system.
The permanently frozen ground makes underground construction extremely difficult.
So outdoor toilets remain common.
At temperatures approaching −50 degrees, even something as simple as using the bathroom becomes a carefully planned activity.
You cannot simply open the door and walk outside wearing normal clothes.
You have to put on warm layers.
Move quickly.
And return inside.
Here, even the most ordinary household tasks are controlled by the weather.
LEARNING FROM FATHER
After returning home, the boy joins his father.
His father is a craftsman.
For generations, traditional skills have been passed from one person to the next.
In Yakutia, some men work with livestock, hunting and traditional crafts.
The father in this family is known for making traditional Yakut knives.
The work requires patience.
There are no shortcuts.
Metal must be shaped carefully.
The blade needs to be balanced.
The handle can be made from locally available wood.
And every movement comes from years of experience.
But the most important part is not the knife.
It is the knowledge.
The father is teaching his son something that was once taught to him.
This is how traditions survive.
Not through books alone.
But through a parent sitting beside a child and saying,
Watch carefully.
Now try it yourself.
THE FIRE NEVER STOPS
As the afternoon disappears, the family returns to one of its most important responsibilities.
The stove.
More wood.
More heat.
Another check of the fire.
In a normal home, heating is something that happens in the background.
Here, heating is part of daily life.
The fire cannot simply be forgotten.
Winter demands attention every single day.
But there is still one more tradition this family looks forward to.
ICE FISHING
When the lakes are frozen thick enough, they become places where families can find food.
For generations, fishing has been part of life in this remote region.
Today, father and son head toward the frozen lake.
The landscape is almost completely silent.
There are no waves.
No boats.
No summer insects.
Just snow, ice and the sound of footsteps.
Beneath them is a thick layer of frozen water.
And beneath that ice, fish are moving through the darkness.
FISHING THROUGH THE ICE
The father knows where to fish.
He has been coming to these waters since he was young.
Now he is bringing his son.
The boy watches carefully.
He is learning another skill that connects him to his environment.
A hole is opened through the ice.
The fishing begins.
There is no guarantee of success.
You wait.
You watch.
You listen.
And you hope.
Then finally, the boy catches a fish.
For him, it is more than food.
It is an achievement.
A memory with his father.
And another lesson about life in Yakutia.
As the afternoon becomes colder, they pack up and begin the journey home.
DINNER FROM THE FROZEN LAKE
Back inside the house, the fish becomes the center of the family's meal.
The mother begins preparing it.
The ingredients are simple.
Fresh fish.
Warm broth.
Basic seasonings.
Nothing complicated.
But after spending hours outside in extreme cold, a warm meal can feel incredibly special.
The family gathers around the table.
The father.
The mother.
The children.
Everyone is finally together again.
Outside, the temperature continues to fall.
Inside, the stove is burning.
The food is warm.
And the family is safe.

THE REAL LIFE OF YAKUTIA
Life here is not easy.
It demands preparation.
Patience.
Strength.
And respect for nature.
But it is also a life filled with community and tradition.
Children grow up understanding the cold.
Parents learn to read the weather.
Families prepare for winter months before the first deep freeze arrives.
And knowledge passes from one generation to the next.
The modern world is reaching Yakutia too.
Phones, internet, modern schools and new opportunities are changing the lives of younger generations.
Some young people leave remote villages to study in larger cities.
Others return.
And some choose to continue the lifestyle their families have followed for generations.
The future may change many things.
But the connection to the land remains powerful.
WHY DO THEY STAY?
So why would anyone choose to live in a place where temperatures can fall to −71 degrees?
Why raise children here?
Why continue living in a landscape where winter can last for months?
Because for the people who call Yakutia home, this is not simply the coldest place on Earth.
It is home.

It is where their grandparents lived.
It is where their traditions were born.
And it is where their memories were created.
The cold may be extreme.

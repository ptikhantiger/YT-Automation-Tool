Every drop of rain that falls on land begins a slow journey back to the sea.

As rainwater runs over rocks and through soil, it dissolves tiny amounts of minerals, mostly sodium and chloride. Rivers carry those minerals downstream, mile after mile, until they reach the ocean.

The rivers themselves taste fresh, because the amount of salt in any single drop is almost nothing. But the ocean has been collecting those drops for billions of years.

Water leaves the sea by evaporation. It rises as vapor, forms clouds, and falls again as rain. The salt cannot evaporate, so it stays behind.

Deep on the ocean floor, volcanic vents add even more minerals, pumping hot, mineral-rich water into the dark.

Over time, the balance settled. Today, seawater is about three and a half percent salt. If you spread all of it across the land, it would form a layer more than one hundred meters thick.

So the ocean is salty for a simple reason. Everything flows into it, and only the water ever leaves.
